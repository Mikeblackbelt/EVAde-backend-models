# Bone Health Metrics

Status labels: **VERIFIED** = bibliographic record and abstract checked; **PARTIAL** = record checked, abstract or numbers not read; **UNVERIFIED** = not confirmed. Numbers are from abstracts.

## 1. Summary

Bone health is measured by areal BMD from DXA, volumetric BMD and finite element (FE) strength from QCT, and microarchitecture from HR-pQCT. In spaceflight, loss is site-dependent (lower limbs, pelvis, and spine lose; the skull gains), is **faster by FE strength than by DXA**, and is **incompletely recovered at one year** in many astronauts. DXA can show recovery while volumetric trabecular bone has not recovered. The first six months after return are the actionable window for reactivating remodeling at sites resorbed in flight. Resistive exercise (ARED) helps cortical bone and areal BMD more than trabecular bone, and the best human evidence for pharmacologic protection (alendronate plus ARED) is small and non-randomized. No portable proxy has been validated for spaceflight bone loss.

For EVAde, the useful output is a **longitudinal trend with uncertainty**, interpreted against the least significant change (LSC) of the measuring system, not a fracture-risk diagnosis.

## 2. Reconciling rate differences

| Measure | Result | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Pooled post-flight change by region | skull +2.2%; thorax and upper limbs -0.7%; lumbar spine and pelvis -6.2%; lower limbs -5.4%; lower-limb loss -0.8% per month | Stavnichuk 2020 |
| FE hip strength | falls 2.0-2.6% per month; DXA total femur BMD loss 0.4-1.8% per month | Keyak 2009 |
| DXA versus QCT recovery | DXA areal BMD recovered in 9 of 10 at 1 year; QCT trabecular BMD incomplete in 5 of 10 | Sibonga 2020 |
| HR-pQCT tibia, return | strength and total vBMD -2.9% to -4.3% | Gabel 2022 (BJSM) |
| HR-pQCT tibia, 12 months | median deficits about 0.9-2.1%; total vBMD -1.4%, 9 of 17 beyond LSC | Gabel 2022 (Sci Rep) |
| Spine | TBS and aBMD can diverge; only ARED plus bisphosphonate preserved spine BMD | Anderson 2026 |

Sites are not directly comparable (distal peripheral versus hip and spine), and pooled rates mix the pre-ARED, iRED, ARED, and bisphosphonate eras, which overstates ARED-era loss.

## 3. Sources: spaceflight bone loss and recovery

- **Stavnichuk et al. (2020).** A systematic review and meta-analysis of bone loss in space travelers. *npj Microgravity*, 6, 13. DOI 10.1038/s41526-020-0103-2. VERIFIED. 25 articles; post-flight density for 148 individuals. Resorption markers rise hyperbolically in flight (plateau about 113% above baseline); formation flat for about 30 days then rises. After landing, resorption falls exponentially and formation rises linearly for 3-5 months. Heterogeneous methods and exercise eras; literature through Nov 2019.
- **Gabel et al. (2022).** Incomplete recovery of bone strength and trabecular microarchitecture at the distal tibia 1 year after return from long duration spaceflight. *Sci Rep*, 12, 9446. DOI 10.1038/s41598-022-13461-1. VERIFIED (numbers via full-text summary; re-check). HR-pQCT, n = 17 (14 men, 3 women), missions 4-7 months. FE failure load -1.3% overall and -3.9% for missions over 6 months at 12 months; incomplete recoverers had higher turnover markers.
- **Gabel et al. (2022).** Pre-flight exercise and bone metabolism predict unloading-induced bone loss due to spaceflight. *Br J Sports Med*, 56(4), 196-203. DOI 10.1136/bjsports-2020-103602. VERIFIED. Mission length and higher pre-flight turnover markers predicted greater loss; larger increases in in-flight lower-body resistance volume predicted preservation of strength.
- **Walle et al. (2024).** Tracking of spaceflight-induced bone remodeling reveals a limited time frame for recovery of resorption sites in humans. *Science Advances*, 10(51), eadq3632. DOI 10.1126/sciadv.adq3632. VERIFIED. 17 astronauts; resorption was 3 times formation over 6 months in flight; 31.8% of bone formed at resorbed sites in the first 6 months after return versus 2.7% at 6-12 months.
- **Matheson et al. (2025).** Recovery of bone microarchitecture and density four years after spaceflight: two case studies. *npj Microgravity*, 11, 47. DOI 10.1038/s41526-025-00511-x. VERIFIED. n = 2; one full recovery, one persisting trabecular deficit offset by cortical thickening.
- **Kemp et al. (2023).** Predicting bone adaptation in astronauts during and after spaceflight. *Life*, 13(11), 2183. DOI 10.3390/life13112183. VERIFIED. A participant-specific model reproduced static morphometry but estimated dynamic rates poorly.
- **Keyak et al. (2009).** Reduction in proximal femoral strength due to long-duration spaceflight. *Bone*, 44(3), 449-453. DOI 10.1016/j.bone.2008.11.014. VERIFIED. n = 13, 4.3-6.5 months; pre-ARED era. No newer astronaut hip FE paper was found (not proven absent).
- **Sibonga et al. (2020).** Use of quantitative computed tomography to assess for clinically-relevant skeletal effects of prolonged spaceflight on astronaut hips. *J Clin Densitom*, 23(2), 155-164. DOI 10.1016/j.jocd.2019.08.005. VERIFIED. n = 10 pilot.
- **Sibonga et al. (2019).** Resistive exercise in astronauts on prolonged spaceflights provides partial protection against spaceflight-induced bone loss. *Bone*, 128, 112037. DOI 10.1016/j.bone.2019.07.013. VERIFIED. ARED preserved femoral neck cortical vBMD and aBMD but not hip trabecular vBMD, and resorption markers were not suppressed.
- **Orwoll et al. (2013).** Skeletal health in long-duration astronauts: nature, assessment, and management recommendations from the NASA Bone Summit. *J Bone Miner Res*, 28(6), 1243-1255. DOI 10.1002/jbmr.1948. VERIFIED (older). Recommended QCT plus FE because of DXA limits; flagged more than 10% hip or spine aBMD loss as a concern.
- **Anderson et al. (2026).** The effects of spaceflight countermeasures on trabecular bone score of the lumbar spine. *Arch Osteoporos*, 21(1), 24. DOI 10.1007/s11657-025-01624-2. VERIFIED. 51 crew; ARED plus bisphosphonate had spine BMD +2.8% from baseline; TBS stayed within baseline for ARED and ARED plus bisphosphonate.
- **Lang et al. (2004).** Cortical and trabecular bone mineral loss from the spine and hip in long-duration spaceflight. *J Bone Miner Res*, 19(6), 1006-1012. Citation verified earlier. QCT, 14 ISS crew on 4-6 month missions. Rates by measure (abstract text from search results; the PubMed page was not fetchable): spine integral vBMD about 0.9%/month; hip integral vBMD 1.2-1.5%/month; hip cortical vBMD 0.4-0.5%/month; hip trabecular vBMD 2.2-2.7%/month; areal BMD 0.9%/month (spine) and 1.4-1.5%/month (hip). A 1.6-1.7%/month cortical figure in a press release is not a vBMD rate. Re-read the paper before quoting a number.
- **Liu et al. (2023)** Bone marrow adiposity after long duration spaceflight, *Nat Commun*, 14, DOI 10.1038/s41467-023-40572-8: PARTIAL (record only).

## 4. Sources: countermeasures and modifiers

- **LeBlanc et al. (2013).** Bisphosphonates as a supplement to exercise to protect bone during long-duration spaceflight. *Osteoporos Int*, 24(7), 2105-2114. DOI 10.1007/s00198-012-2243-z. VERIFIED. 7 astronauts on alendronate versus 18 on iRED and 11 on ARED; ARED plus alendronate attenuated decline in nearly all indices. Small, non-randomized, historical comparators.
- **Smith et al. (2012).** Benefits for bone from resistance exercise and nutrition in long-duration spaceflight. *J Bone Miner Res*, 27(9), 1896-1906. DOI 10.1002/jbmr.1647. VERIFIED. ARED crew (n = 5) kept hip BMD unchanged; resorption markers rose in both groups.
- **Zwart et al. (2018).** Dietary acid load and bone turnover during long-duration spaceflight and bed rest. *Am J Clin Nutr*, 107(5), 834-844. DOI 10.1093/ajcn/nqy029. VERIFIED. Estimated net endogenous acid production in flight associated with post-mission lumbar spine loss; sodium associated with urinary calcium.
- **Smith et al. (2014).** Men and women in space: bone loss and kidney stone risk after long-duration spaceflight. *J Bone Miner Res*, 29(7), 1639-1645. DOI 10.1002/jbmr.2185. VERIFIED. 42 astronauts (9 women); responses were the same by sex; stone risk typically rose after flight.
- **Scott et al. (2023).** Effects of exercise countermeasures on multisystem function in long duration spaceflight astronauts. *npj Microgravity*, 9, 11. DOI 10.1038/s41526-023-00256-5. VERIFIED. N = 46; wide individual variability (change -30% to +5%); not bone-specific.
- **Urinary calcium.** Ren et al. (2020), *Aerosp Med Hum Perform*, 91(9), 689-696, DOI 10.3357/AMHP.5606.2020 (VERIFIED; 6 subjects; averaging repeated measures needed to detect expected rises). Buckey, Thamer & Lan (2023), *Curr Opin Nephrol Hypertens*, 32(2), 172-176, DOI 10.1097/MNH.0000000000000863 (VERIFIED). Thamer & Buckey (2022), DOI 10.3357/AMHP.5979.2022 (PARTIAL).
- **Artificial gravity.** Clement et al. (2022), AGBRESA protocol, *Front Physiol*, 13, 976926, DOI 10.3389/fphys.2022.976926 (VERIFIED protocol only; no bone-outcome paper found). Do not model artificial gravity as a bone countermeasure.
- **Vitamin D:** no spaceflight-specific trial found; evidence quality unverified. Turnover marker basics: Schini et al. (2023), *Endocr Rev*, 44(3), 417-473, DOI 10.1210/endrev/bnac031 (PARTIAL).
- **Radiation and bone.** No human 2019+ evidence linking spaceflight radiation dose to astronaut bone outcomes was found. Kim et al. (2021), *Int J Mol Sci*, 22(21), 11711, DOI 10.3390/ijms222111711 (VERIFIED): 0.5 Gy simulated GCR caused femoral trabecular loss in mice. Rodent data at doses and geometries that may not match crew exposure.

## 5. Sources: portable and non-DXA metrics

- **REMS.** Cortet et al. (2021), *Bone*, 143, 115786, DOI 10.1016/j.bone.2020.115786 (VERIFIED): 4,307 women; femoral neck sensitivity 90.4% and specificity 95.5% versus DXA; Caucasian women only; device-linked investigators (conflict not checked). Adami et al. (2020), *Bone*, 134, 115297, DOI 10.1016/j.bone.2020.115297 (VERIFIED; 5-year fracture follow-up). Fuggle et al. (2024), *Aging Clin Exp Res*, 36, 135, DOI 10.1007/s40520-024-02784-w (VERIFIED; society consensus piece). Emerging, with industry-adjacent evidence; no microgravity data.
- **Heel QUS.** Metrailler et al. (2023), *Osteoporos Int*, 34(8), 1401-1409, DOI 10.1007/s00198-023-06728-4 (VERIFIED): predicts incident fractures independently of TBS, BMD, and FRAX (women, 6.7 years); no evidence that it tracks short-term change.
- **Opportunistic CT.** Mousavi et al. (2025), *Clin Imaging*, 118, 110372, DOI 10.1016/j.clinimag.2024.110372 (VERIFIED): 41 studies, pooled correlation with DXA 0.59, AUC 0.81. Pickhardt et al. (2019), *Br J Radiol*, 92(1094), 20180726, DOI 10.1259/bjr.20180726 (VERIFIED): 1,603 adults, mean annual loss -2.0% in women and -1.0% in men.
- **Deep learning on radiographs.** Hsieh et al. (2021), *Nat Commun*, 12, 5472, DOI 10.1038/s41467-021-25779-x (VERIFIED): hip osteoporosis AUPRC 0.89; cross-sectional, single-system.
- **Wearable and loading indices:** no validated wearable estimating adult BMD or fracture risk was found; treat loading exposure (impacts, ARED volume) as an exposure variable, not a bone measurement.

## 7. Implementation steps

1. Adopt a minimal metric set: DXA spine and hip aBMD, TBS, QCT or HR-pQCT where available, a resorption and a formation marker, and urinary calcium; document that sources use different units and sites.
2. Define "meaningful change" as exceeding the site and scanner-specific least significant change, not a fixed percent.
3. Build two recovery flags, areal (DXA) and volumetric or trabecular (QCT or HR-pQCT), since they disagree.
4. Seed trend priors from Stavnichuk (rates and marker kinetics) and show intervals, not a single mean.
5. Record covariates: mission duration, ARED volume, pre-flight turnover markers, sodium and acid-load surrogates, urinary calcium, sex.
6. Use first-void urinary calcium averaged over 2-3 weeks as the primary portable chemical trend signal, with the Ren 2020 reproducibility limits.
7. Treat REMS and heel QUS as exploratory cross-sectional proxies; require side-by-side DXA validation in the project cohort.
8. Reserve opportunistic CT and radiograph ML for pre- and post-flight imaging.
9. Plan post-landing follow-up concentrated in the first 6 months, with 12- and 24-month checks where trabecular deficits persist.
10. Use NHANES and UK Biobank only for reference distributions; keep FRAX outside the trend model.
11. Start NASA data-access paperwork early (see [data-collection-sources](../data-collection-sources/bone-and-spaceflight.md)).

## 8. Evaluation and robustness

- **Metrics:** agreement of any proxy with DXA or QCT (correlation and Bland-Altman); precision error and LSC for repeated measures; person-level trend-detection sensitivity.
- **Robustness checks:** same-day and across-week repeat reliability; sensitivity to positioning, hydration, and device calibration drift.
- **Covariates:** age, sex, body size, exercise, medication.

## 9. Limitations and open questions

- Cohorts are small (HR-pQCT n = 17, QCT hips n = 10-14); rates may not extrapolate to longer missions or other countermeasure regimens.
- No portable proxy is validated against spaceflight bone loss, and no in-flight CTX and P1NP time course was separated from pooled markers.
- No vitamin D trial in crew, no AGBRESA bone-outcome paper, and no human radiation-bone evidence was found.
- Which "Kanis 2008 FRAX" DOI is correct? Full author lists were truncated for several papers and should be retrieved before a final bibliography.
